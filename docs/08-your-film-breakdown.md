- **Coaching read:** if the goalie is set and square before your release, the shot has to beat him with placement or deception, not velocity. ## What to film so I *can* break down your mechanics

This is the platform's own checklist, and now you have a personal reason to follow it:

1. **Side angle.** Phone at hip height, ~10 feet away, perpendicular to your shooting line. This is the money angle — weight transfer, flex and posture all live here.
2. **Front angle.** Same distance, whole body in frame. Hand separation and blade angle need this one.
3. **Five shots minimum, same type.** One rep tells you nothing. Five tells you what's habitual.
4. **Good light.** A dim garage hides exactly the thing you're trying to see.
5. **Slow the phone down, not the edit.** If your phone shoots 240fps slo-mo, use it. Real high-frame-rate footage beats any post-production slowdown, because the frames are real.
6. **Film the same five shots every 30 days.** Same angle, same distance, same lighting. That's what makes a before/after undeniable.
7. **Always keep five seconds of lead-in.** Applies to game exports too. The release is the part everyone trims off.

Do that once and I'll score all seven points properly and build you a real annotated breakdown — which then becomes the sample breakdown on the sales page, which is the highest-converting asset the business can have.

## The Connor Bedard clip — don't use this one

That file is a third-party social edit containing NHL broadcast footage, Maple Leafs and Blackhawks game clips, and interview footage. I have not put it anywhere near the site, and I'd recommend you don't either.

**The problem:** NHL broadcast footage is owned by the NHL and its broadcast partners. The edit itself is owned by whoever made it. Using either inside a **paid membership product** is materially different from posting a reaction on TikTok. It's the difference between commentary and commercial use, and the NHL enforces this actively.

Connor Bedard's name and likeness are also separately protected. Using a named NHL player to sell a subscription implies endorsement, which is its own exposure — and it's the kind of thing that gets a young business a letter before it gets a customer.
