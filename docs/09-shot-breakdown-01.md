## The headline finding

> **Your flex is the best thing in the clip. Your timing is what's costing you.**

This matters because it inverts the obvious fix. The instinct when a shot isn't beating goalies is *train for a harder shot* — more reps, heavier stick, more power. Your film says you already generate power well. The shaft bow at frame 37 proves the energy is there.

What the film also says is that you **announce the shot about half a second early**. Eight tenths of a second of puck drag is a long time for a goalie who is already square. It links straight back to the game clip: the goalie was set and tracking before the release. That isn't bad luck. That's a readable setup.

**Shorten the runway, keep the flex, and the same shot starts beating people.**

## The back-leg note

The trailing leg going fully airborne is a pad-and-sneakers pattern. Concrete gives you something to push off that ice doesn't, and there's no edge to hold. Don't panic about it — but don't reinforce it either, because on skates that same motion becomes falling away from the shot, which leaks the power you just loaded.

Watch for it specifically in your next on-ice reps.

## Prescribed — next 30 days

| Drill | Fixes | Prescription |
|---|---|---|
| **Quick Release Ladder** | Points 3, 4 | 5 × 8 shots. Cut the drag distance every set. |
| **Puck Position Markers** | Point 2 | 3 × 15. Maintains the flex you already have while the drag shortens. |
| **Eyes Up Shooting** | Points 3, 6 | 4 × 10. Call the corner out loud *before* release. |

**Constraint that does most of the work:** put a tape line on the pad and don't let the puck travel past it before release. Start where you are now and move the line in a couple of inches a week. You're training the same shot to happen in less runway.
